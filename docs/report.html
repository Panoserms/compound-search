Compound Search

Do stemming and compound splitting still help German search, and is anything left of that help once a neural model is added?

Recall@10 on 1,025 test questions: how often the right passage is among the first ten results. Four of six hypotheses were supported.

1. What the project is

  • The problem. German glues nouns together. A question asks for the Einwohnerdichte of Melbourne; the passage that answers it says Einwohnerzahl and Bevölkerungsdichte. A keyword search finds the city's name and nothing for the thing that is asked about.
  • The classic fix. Reduce words to their stems and cut compounds into their parts.
  • The open question. Search engines now also use neural ("dense") models, which compare meaning instead of words. Does the old linguistic work still pay once such a model is in the system?
  • What the study does. It tests 10 systems, from plain keyword search to a hybrid with an LLM, against six hypotheses that were written down before the systems existed.

2. How it was set up

  • Data: GermanDPR (CC BY 4.0), German Wikipedia.
    • 26,372 passages to search in.
    • 1,000 development questions on 778 gold passages. Every choice was made here.
    • 1,025 test questions on 192 gold passages, held out until the configuration was frozen.
  • Two pools. GermanDPR ships three trap passages with every question, picked with BM25.
    • Under plain BM25, one of them outranks the right passage for 76% of the development questions.
    • Headline pool: each question is ranked without its own three traps. This was fixed before any comparison system was built.
    • Pool as shipped: reported beside the headline for every system.
  • Fixed in advance, never tuned: BM25 with its standard settings; the dense model multilingual-e5-small as published; rank fusion with its standard constant and no weights.
  • Statistics:
    • Many questions share one gold passage, so intervals and tests resample passages, not questions.
    • 95% bootstrap intervals; paired randomisation tests; Holm correction across the six tests.
  • Everything runs on one 8 GB laptop. No paid API. The LLM is a local Gemma model.

3. The systems and the test result

  • Keyword search. Stems lift plain BM25 from 70.8% to 77.2%; split compounds lift it further to 81.4%.
  • Dense model alone. 92.4%, without any of this work.
  • Hybrids. Fusing the dense model with plain BM25 gives 88.9%, which is worse than the dense model alone. Fusing it with the stemmed and split BM25 gives 92.4%.
  • LLM rewriting. A local model adds search terms to the queries on which the two retrievers disagree: 93.2%.
    • The rule that picks these queries looks at rankings and scores only, never at the answer.
    • It sent 5.3% of the development questions and 6.5% of the test questions to the LLM (67 of 1,025; 83 under at least one of the two pools).

4. The six hypotheses

  • Supported: H1, H2, H3, H4b.
    • Stems and splitting help BM25, and by more than predicted (+6.3 and +4.2 points).
    • The help survives inside the hybrid: +3.5 points. This was the main question.
  • Not supported: H4a. The hybrid does not beat the dense model alone: 45 questions won, 45 lost.
  • Not supported: H5. LLM rewriting gains +0.8 points (10 questions won, 2 lost), which does not pass the corrected test.
  • A weaker second view. With the pool as shipped, the gain inside the hybrid is +2.7 points and does not pass the corrected test (Holm p = 0.076).
What an engineer could take from it, on this data: use the dense model. If you must stay with keyword search, stem and split. If you fuse the two, do the linguistic work first, because fusing with plain BM25 makes the dense model worse.

5. The compound splitter by itself

  • How it works. It needs no dictionary. It counts the words of the passages it is going to index. A word may be cut into nouns it has seen, and the cut wins if its parts are, on average, more frequent than the whole word.
    • Linking elements are handled: Bundes|verfassungs|gericht.
    • A frequent fixed word such as Bahnhof stays whole.
  • How it was scored. 100 capitalised words from development questions, labelled by hand by the author on a page that shows nothing a splitter did. HanTa, an open morphology tool, is scored beside it.

  • A win. "Wie hoch ist die Einwohnerdichte in Melbourne?": rank 43 with plain words, rank 50 with stems, rank 1 with split compounds.
  • A loss. "Welches Unternehmen betreibt die Straßenbahn in Straßburg?": rank 3 with stems, rank 12 once Straßburg is cut into Straße and Burg.
  • Another loss. "Wer ist Gabe Newell?": rank 1 with stems, rank 12 once Newell is cut into new and ell.
  • Why the linguistic steps still matter in the hybrid. Rank fusion rewards passages that both lists contain. If plain BM25 misses the right passage, that passage has only its dense rank, and passages found by both lists overtake it.

7. What was checked

  • The test run. The results come from one search of the test questions. The unchanged systems were later run on them twice more, only to check that the results repeat; both repeats gave identical reports.
  • A clean copy. On 12 November 2026 the whole study was repeated in a copy with a new environment, a fresh download and freshly computed embeddings. All 33 result files were identical to the saved ones. This was before the hand labels were added; the label scores are not part of that check.
  • The write-up.
    • Tables and charts are generated from the result files.
    • An automated test looks up every rank, count and example that the README quotes.
    • An earlier draft contained statements the data did not back. They were replaced, and each correction is dated in HYPOTHESES.md.

8. Limits

  • One dataset. The questions were written by people who had the passage in front of them. Real search queries are shorter and messier.
  • A small pool. 26,372 passages. Among millions, the balance between keyword search and a dense model may differ.
  • One gold passage per question. Other passages that also answer it count as misses.
  • The choice of pool matters. The main result passes the corrected test in the headline pool only.
  • One dense model, one LLM, one prompt, all small. A larger LLM or a weighted fusion could change the two results that failed.
  • The compound labels are one person's, on 100 words, and were revised once after the scores were known.
  • The hypotheses were fixed in a local file, not in a public registry. Its dates rest on file timestamps.

9. The headline number

+21.6 points Recall@10 over plain BM25 on 1,025 German questions (92.4% vs. 70.8% on GermanDPR test, headline pool; linguistic preprocessing adds +3.5 points inside the RRF hybrid).
  • The protocol fixed this wording before the test run, for the system with the best development score. That system is the dense model alone (94.9% on the development set).
  • For the full pipeline the gain is +22.3 points. Its last 0.8 points come from the LLM step, whose hypothesis was not supported.
